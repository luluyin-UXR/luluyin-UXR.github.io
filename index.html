Senior UX Researcher · 2024 to 2025

The Setting

At banks, building business journeys is a core task for the Servicing and OKYC product teams.

Journeys
Also called workflows or business processes — they define how a task, such as customer onboarding, runs from start to finish.
Journey Building
Defining the steps of onboarding, how they connect, and how the bank's systems process them.

Research Questions

Assumption: Journey building relies heavily on engineering, creating significant time and budget constraints. Product decision: a no-code configuration tool that lets users build and manage journeys with minimal engineering support.

  1. How do customers build business processes today?
  2. How do they collaborate with others?
  3. What challenges and workarounds exist today?
  4. Which tools do they use?
  5. What would a new solution need for successful adoption?

In-depth Research for a Tier 1 UK Bank — Commercial Banking Platform
Uncovering redesign opportunities for a platform serving corporates, governments and financial institutions · Capco, London
Heuristic Evaluation Stakeholder Interviews Customer Interviews Large-scale Survey Commercial Banking B2B
UX Researcher · Capco · 2022

The Setting

A Tier 1 UK bank's commercial banking platform helps finance teams manage their organisation's money day to day. This bank is one of Capco's most important clients. An R&D team was set up to deliver a platform redesign — the research goal was to ground that redesign in real user behaviour and experience.

Research Goal

"How do users actually work with the platform, and where does the experience create friction?"

The challenge was to move beyond a broad improvement mandate and identify where investment would create the most value — for both the business and its customers.

Challenge and Approach

Scope
"Improve the overall platform" — a broad starting brief with no clear priority
Original Brief
Refined Focus
Where should investment focus to create the most value for the business and its users?
Research Question
Trade-off
Limited engineering support ruled out a platform-wide overhaul — prioritise the most critical business and user challenges
Constraint and Decision

Stage 1 · Internal Discovery

Built the business view of the platform before going to users.

Heuristic Evaluation Stakeholder Interviews

Outputs: evidence framework of platform issues, validated patterns from the business perspective.

Stage 2 · User Research

Captured the user view across daily tasks, team roles, collaboration and friction.

1-2-1 Customer Interviews Large-scale Survey

Outputs: quantified recurring themes, validated behavioural patterns, jobs-to-be-done across team types.

Finding 1 · The Shared Pain Point

Account setup emerged as the critical issue on both sides. For the bank, it consumed significant time and effort from staff supporting customers. For users, it caused disjointed journeys, slower tasks and risk in managing day-to-day finance work — a structural problem, not just a usability one.

Finding 2 · Same Jobs, Different Ways of Working

Both team types used the platform for the same core tasks — but had fundamentally different needs that one rigid setup could not serve.

Small Finance Teams
Priority: flexible access for every member
"Everyone needs to be able to do everything when needed."
Large Finance Teams
Priority: specialised roles and stronger controls
"We need tight control over who can approve what."
The problem: Both were served by the same rigid setup. The platform was not flexible enough to support different ways of working — a fundamental design gap, not a surface-level issue.

Recommendations

01
Rethink Account Setup

Redesign user-account and permission management to be adaptable to different team structures — flexible access with the governance finance work needs.

02
Broader UI Refresh

Address usability and consistency issues surfaced in research; modernise the overall experience.

Impact

Framing Shift
"Address a broader set of issues" → "Focus on the most critical issue and make the platform adaptable"
Client side: clear trade-offs and priorities, broader teams aligned on a user-informed direction, timely delivery with limited engineering, reduced customer support effort.
User side: streamlined user journeys, better overall experience, more efficient day-to-day work.

The Keeper of Paintings, National Gallery AR Experience
Cross-cultural UX research programme for a first-of-its-kind immersive gallery experience · Brunel University London x The National Gallery
Desk ResearchPractitioner Interviews (14)Parent Surveys (50)Children Focus Groups (24)Co-design WorkshopsField Testing
UX Researcher · Brunel · 2019 to 2022

The Project

A research and development initiative co-led by StoryFutures at Brunel University and The National Gallery, the first time the Gallery had applied digital immersive technology to its on-site visitor experience. Seven institutions from the UK and China collaborated to create a child-friendly experience that worked meaningfully across both markets. My role was to serve as the bridge between the UK and China teams, facilitating mutual understanding of markets, audience behaviour, production workflows and creative goals across two very different institutional cultures.

Research Questions

  • Which immersive cultural experiences are most successful globally, and what makes them work?
  • How do UK and Chinese cultural production teams approach immersive content differently?
  • What are the primary interests and preferences of children and families in both countries?
  • What type of experience should the National Gallery create to genuinely increase visitor engagement?

Three Research Phases

Phase 1
Competitive Landscape
Desk research on global immersive cultural experiences and practitioner interviews with 14 production experts across the UK and China.
Phase 2
Audience Landscape
Parent surveys with 50 families exploring children's museum habits and AR familiarity, plus focus groups with 24 children on interests and painting preferences.
Phase 3
Design Areas
Co-design workshops with children to shape the concept, then a field study testing the AR experience through observation, post-session discussion and usability surveys.

Testing Results, Experience Dimensions

Key Audience Insights

  • Children in both the UK and China had limited AR experience but strong enthusiasm for it once introduced.
  • Mobile and tablet games were strongly preferred over PC-based games, informing the decision to build a mobile-first AR experience.
  • Art galleries were perceived as less engaging than science museums because of static, non-interactive displays, a direct creative brief.
  • Boys were drawn to level progression and rewards; girls preferred avatar customisation. The final concept accommodated both without compromising either.

Cross-Cultural Collaboration Insight

UK teams prioritised contracts and research before moving into production. Chinese teams focused more on pre-sales and operated at a faster pace. Bridging these two working cultures required active facilitation of shared creative and strategic goals at every stage and a genuine investment in mutual understanding rather than assuming alignment.

Final Concept
The Keeper of Paintings

An interactive, gamified AR mobile app combining progression levels and a reward system. A neutral guide character, the Keeper of Paintings, leads children through an immersive journey to explore and appreciate artworks at the National Gallery. The story begins with the Keeper losing a magical colour palette. Visitors follow clues hidden inside the paintings to recover it, earning rewards along the way and ultimately earning the title of the new Keeper of Paintings.

Gamified AR mobile experienceCross-cultural designChild-friendly narrativeProgressive reward systemNational Gallery launch
Outcome

The experience launched at the National Gallery and was adopted as a permanent part of its family visitor offering. The cross-cultural research framework was published as part of the StoryFutures programme. View the project at nationalgallery.org.uk →

The Project

Understand what helps or stops first-time shoppers completing a purchase on the Drunk Elephant website — across three journey stages: browse, view product, and checkout.

Research Questions
Overall · Users' impressions of the website

Key Findings

Browse & Find
Findability friction

Size and quantity choices reset between pages, forcing repeat selections. Look-alike variants confused first-time shoppers. Navigation felt hidden and some dropdowns "spammy."

🔁 Selections didn't persist across pages
👥 Variants too similar to differentiate
🗂️ Navigation hidden or clunky
View Product
Missing "Why?"

Users needed to know what the product contains, how it works, and whether it suits them — none of this was easy to find.

🖼️ Product images cut off or drag-to-scroll
📋 Ingredients buried below marketing copy
❓ "Why is this one different?" unanswered
Checkout
Technical blockers

Critical bugs stopped most participants completing the task — making checkout the lowest-rated stage.

📍 Address confirmed then replaced by checkout
💳 Card number field rejected all input
🚫 Task completion prevented entirely

Recommendations

Priority 1 — Highest

Address critical experience issues causing task failures.

  • Fix tech blockers: image loading failures, address mismatch in checkout, card number field not accepting input
  • Consistent product choices: carry selected size and quantity across filters, thumbnails and product pages
  • Redesign product page: improve image gallery (swipe/slide, not drag), surface ingredients and benefits higher, reduce cross-selling clutter
Priority 2 — Medium

Address key usability issues making tasks less efficient.

  • Improve findability: make navigation menu more visible and intuitive, balancing collapsed and expanded views
  • Visual distinction: make products easier to tell apart on listing page (larger names, stronger visual hierarchy)
  • Responsive design: ensure pages load and display correctly across different screen sizes and browsers
Priority 3 — Lower

Further improve overall experience, engagement and brand perception.

  • Experience tone: move from social-media style to a more professional, caring tone to strengthen brand credibility
  • Next steps: prioritise improvements by user impact and implementation effort, define success metrics per area, test refined designs with users
